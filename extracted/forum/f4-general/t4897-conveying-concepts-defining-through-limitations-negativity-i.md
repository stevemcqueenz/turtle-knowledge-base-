# Conveying Concepts & Defining Through Limitations & Negativity - Itemization

## Post 29511 by Mativh (Patch Note Conspiracy Theorist) — 2022-11-19T17:31:00Z
<!-- url: https://forum.turtlecraft.gg/viewtopic.php?p=29511#p29511 | page 1 | era: pre-1.18.1 -->

Itemization;
In Vanilla WoW there are often negative stats on items, unlike in newer WoW.
Not only increase of power matters, but also a decrease, a tradeoff where it makes sense, to better put into practice a concept that item is ment to signify.
It's important to acknowledge that items in roleplaying games give stats not only because of the physical qualities of the item, but because they are tools to signify the state of the character, convey a story, assume a situation/experience that character interracted with.
For example:
[The Ring of Scorn](https://www.wowhead.com/classic/item=3235/ring-of-scorn)
Which is a reward from a Forsaken quest which has a suprpising depth to it, and when you're wearing this ring, you feel the scorn of that person who gave it to you, because you've been through this experience with your character, and it makes you a little more stoic hence the stamina bonus, but at the same time somewhat bleak, hence the lower spirit.
In this case the item is conveying a mental state that affects your body, but there could be the opposite, items directly causing negative physical effects because of something related to the state of the item, while lifting up attributes because of psychological reasons. And of course there are concepts improving one aspect of mental state, while lowering another, improving physique in one way while hindering in another, granting knowledge in one form of magic while affecting the resistance to another, and various combinations of these tradeoffs.
Vanilla WoW because of this has deeper and more interesting itemization than newer WoW, and is more a roleplaying game.
I'm glad to see Turtle WoW following the vanilla itemization design philosophy.

Early WoW understood the importance of defining and creating meaning through limitations and negativity (Although there was some standardization compared to W3). Not only lifting all restrictions and giving boosts like later WoW, where it borders with toxic positivity. If everything is standardized, boosted, deconstructed, nothing will make sense anymore.
So there should be perhaps a more open discussion about not only what to add or unrestrict, but also the opposite, if it enhances the vanilla experience and makes the world more lore-adequate, not only when it comes to items, but everything else.

Historically speaking, no, early WoW lifted many of the limitations that were placed on the other MMOs that came before it.  There's a reason WoW was called "babby's first mmo" at the time of its release, because it came along a made things a lot easier with its theme park mmo design.

You compare Vanilla WoW to later WoW, but that's irrelevant to the design of Vanilla WoW.  Vanilla WoW wasn't inspired by later WoW, it's actually the other way around.  What Vanilla WoW was inspired by were contemporary MMOs of the time, games like EverQuest.  EverQuest imposed many, many more limitations on the players, especially in its earliest iterations.  Travel was a lot harder on the players since you had no flight paths to breeze you along to the next zone, you had to carry food and water on you at all times or else you'd become thirsty and hungry and stop regenerating health and mana, most classes couldn't effectively solo so you were reliant on grouping with other players, items and even the coins you carried had weight to them and if you went over your carry limit (as determined by your Strength) you'd become encumbered and move slowly (to the point where you'd stop moving altogether hilariously), coins didn't automatically round up so you'd have thousands of copper pieces that would weigh you down, hybrid classes took an experience penalty and that experience penalty was shared when you grouped with them, you lost experience when you died, and when you died you had to go on a corpse run to get your stuff back and if you didn't get back to your stuff in time you lost your stuff and oh yeah you could die on a corpse run and it was really easy to do so because you were running around naked, spells had to be memorized and you only had like eight spell slots so you could only have that many spells ready at one time, if you wanted to regenerate mana with any swiftness you had to meditate which meant sitting down and opening your spellbook which covered your entire screen so good luck, there were tons of high leveled enemy mobs placed in starter areas, if your race didn't have a form of nightvision you couldn't see anything at night, quests had to be accessed by typing the exact right phrases to an NPC who didn't have some big exclamation mark over his head, and the list goes on and on and on.

I bring EverQuest up only because the developers were painfully aware how that specific game played.  A number of the WoW devs were themselves EverQuest players.  Jeffrey Kaplan and Alex Afrasiabi were both well known and outspoken EverQuest players.  They knew what MMOs were like at the time firsthand and chose to lift the restrictions placed upon MMOs.  Immersion and reality took a backseat to gameplay and accessibility in Vanilla WoW.  That's the Vanilla WoW design.  Not just in gameplay, the art design went from the more realistic designs of other MMOs and early alpha WoW to a cartoonish unrealistic design.  But hey, that design worked, as it created the most popular MMO of all time.  And if the current iteration of WoW is even more simplified, it only got that way thanks to Vanilla WoW.  Vanilla WoW paved that road.

I get that you want to make the game more immersive.  More power to you.  But don't call that the Vanilla WoW design because it wasn't.
