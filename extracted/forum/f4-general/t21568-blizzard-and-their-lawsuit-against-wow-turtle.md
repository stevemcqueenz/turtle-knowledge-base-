I mean, the question is, how is TWoW actually stealing anything of Blizzard's? The game is publicly available. They transformed it, modified it, and then redistributed it, for free, as an derivative of the original. The server software is reverse engineered with open source technology.

The argument is, "It's ours, we made it, we don't want anyone to create derivative works." But if European law allows for derivative works of what is effectively abandonware (because Blizzard *does not* produce and distribute version 1.12 of the game anymore) than Blizzard has an uphill battle.

Blizzards entire marketing and eco system has revolved around fan art. BlizzCon itself is a celebration of derivative fan art. Now Blizzard is saying, "No, because this fan art is a video game, we are going to sue you."

At some point, when you foster, encourage, and promote a community around your products encouraging people to create derivative works, how can you continue to claim ownership of the creative work of others?

Don't get me wrong, I fully understand the nonsense that is the current copyright and trademark laws in the United States, and to a lesser extent, Europe. I also understand the existence of SOME law is good. But it's current state is predatory and exploitative, not protective, and needs to change.

I think the developers at Turtle WoW are so clever that they can create their own game with these races and lore since Blizzard also drew from mythology and didn't ask anyone if they could use them in their game.
All of these races existed in fantasy literature or mythologies long before Blizzard used them in the world of Warcraft and later in World of Warcraft. The game didn't claim them, rather it drew from older sources and adapted them according to its own aesthetics.

A brief overview of the origins:

Orcs - Their roots go back to the work of J. R. R. Tolkien (The Lord of the Rings), where they were demonic, cruel creatures serving dark lords. Tolkien inspired them with the Old English word orc (from the Latin Orcus - underworld).

Goblins - They already appear in European folklore of the Middle Ages as small, mischievous and insidious creatures. Tolkien also used them as a synonym for orcs (especially in The Hobbit).

Elves – The oldest mentions are in Norse mythology (álfar), where elves are supernatural beings connected to nature. The modern image of "noble, long-lived beings" was developed mainly by Tolkien.

Trolls – They come from Scandinavian folklore. Traditionally, they were giant, often stupid, but strong creatures living in mountains or caves.

Tauren – This is an exception. "Tauren" does not appear directly in literature or mythology. Blizzard created them as an original race, but they were based on a combination of Native American culture and mythological motifs of the minotaur (Greek mythology: a man with a bull's head).

In other words, Blizzard took most of them from existing folklore and fantasy literature, but reshaped their character and cultural background to fit into its world. The Tauren are practically the only race you mentioned that can be considered a Blizzard original (even if inspired by other myths).
