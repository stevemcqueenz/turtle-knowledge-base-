# Auctionhouse rudeprices 4 newplayers

## Post 140416 by Cosmico (Patch Note Conspiracy Theorist) — 2025-07-19T07:14:00Z
<!-- url: https://forum.turtlecraft.gg/viewtopic.php?p=140416#p140416 | page 1 | era: pre-1.18.1 -->

Greetings from the South seas server!

I hope you're all having a fantastic day and that your journey was fruitful. I'll take this opportunity to mention the fantastic opportunity we have to experience Turtle in its current state, with its daring adventures and new perspectives on the classic game, especially for those of us who never had the chance to play the vanilla version.

Hoping you enjoy this topic, I'll proceed with the context...

During all the time I've been working with Turtle until now, the auction house has never been an important ally, so to speak, for leveling up, if we consider it in conjunction with the progressive progression of the different game mechanics and the diverse game types that exist (PVP, dungeons, quests, area leveling and professions, etc.). But I want to contribute a little bit about my experience, which wasn't so pleasant either, as it's something I've seen firsthand, something no one has told me: simply, for 50 levels, I was never able to buy a single piece of equipment that could improve my leveling or give me an advantage. Oh, and I want to clarify that the green trinket found in the market is the only halfway affordable item one can buy past level 20, and this is provided one decides not to invest that much in other areas of the game, such as skills.

Today, however, I've reached the point. My experience as part of the professional community has shown me that the problem isn't the production, but the auction itself. Let me explain...

A guy approached me to ask for the price of a potion... and since I'd had previous experience, I was cautious about getting him excited about the possibility of being able to fulfill his request... so I headed to the auction house only to realize that there were only 5 or 6 units of the herb needed for the potion for 80 silver, which was "arcane elixir." It's the herb "blindweed." To my surprise, each unit of this herb costs on average from 90 silver to 1 gold, or more. What I'm trying to explain is that producing a final product is more expensive than buying it directly from the market, and this inevitably affects players who level up, as the prices of final products on the market are unaffordable for them. Let me expand on this idea a little further: up until level 30, the only potions or boosts I could afford were those obtained as a result of dungeon crawls, as I was never able to access the ingredients at a reasonable price or in any quantity, since I started by questing, rather than leveling from level 1, which was the leveling path I took at the time.
Consequently, I was unable to produce the requested potion and had no choice but to cancel the order. This affected me because, while leveling, I never considered using a profession player. My only attempt to do so through an enchanter led me to the conclusion that only a level 60 player could afford enchantments due to the high cost of the materials required to produce them. For this reason, I assumed that no profession was a viable alternative to the auction house. In conclusion, not only was the auction house almost entirely absent during my leveling process, but my perception of the auction house also prevented me from gaining any understanding of the profession system. Now that I've reached level 50, I find myself unable to supply support items to low-level players, as auction prices are geared, even for low-impact end products, exclusively to the benefit of the max-level economy, or at least from level 50 to 60, when you already have at least an advanced character with at least one profession and two or three maxed-out secondary professions. This doesn't apply to new players.
I later went to the game chats to express my feelings of anguish in the face of this situation, which led me to redefine my own self-perception of the hopelessness that comes from approaching the game from ignorance due to inability rather than lack of skill. I encountered phrases like "you don't understand the auction," or "the new player learning about the auction," and the one that confused me the most was "that's how supply and demand works," since this is a fallacy by association, given that the issue at hand isn't the request for an item, but the context that encompasses that item and determines the request—in this case, inaccessibility.

What generates this condition?

To begin with, I'll venture, despite only being level 50, to make a bold assumption. Apparently, basic items and necessities up to 10 or 20 levels below your current level are only accessible at that level (10 or 20 levels higher), resulting in the inability to access profession items during those levels, commissioned by a player with an already developed profession. I could say here that this is frustrating for your performance at your current level, but that's not the point. Rather, it's the complete absence of well-planned, planned, and executed gameplay mechanics. Simply put, at your level, you should be able to access items that are required at your level, rather than hunting for items that may have been useful to you 10 or 20 levels ago, but you were completely unaware of their application and your relationship to them, due to the high prices of the materials required to produce them.

My conclusion is that a possible maximum is needed for both profession ingredients and final products. I'm talking about a maximum at point "a1" for an ingredient, and a maximum at point "a6" or maybe "b2" (trying to conceptualize progress distance). That is, the item needed to produce a thing has a price that goes up to, for example, 100 silver, and the product produced by it (taking into account that some final products require more than one element of the same type) can only reach a maximum of perhaps 10 or 20 times the value of the ingredient or component, but no more than that point, and by point I mean the point at which the player of that level can no longer obtain it in any possible way.

I briefly explained this approach in the in-game chat, and someone simply questioned me, saying something like, "What? Price controls?" And that unleashed a wave of negative comments against the very unfavorable situation we're trying to address here. Some of these comments, or the vast majority, were simply displays of humor, but some weren't. I want to leave you with this expression I've been using since I arrived here: "It's not possible that a person wants to become a millionaire by selling just one pair of pants." What I mean is, it's absurd to say that the selling price of something should be a right superior to the economic and practical-technical function for which it's all designed: to create homogeneous gameplay with many sources of possibilities to enrich the game. Failing to achieve this is like trying to defend the dysfunctional performance of a concept already implemented to yield positive results, which, as we're arguing here, doesn't seem to be the case.

And I urge you not to take me out of context. I have incredibly enjoyed the game with all its unique facets and perspectives, which reflect great development and a perspective of dedication. However, all that effort and spirit is overshadowed by the mechanical, and perhaps unintentional, bias reflected in the auction house's numbers. A bias that makes it impossible for this system to function as planned because it either enables or leaves the door open for excessive pricing for materials and ingredients.

With nothing further to add, I bid farewell and hope that a reasonable understanding can be reached on this matter.
