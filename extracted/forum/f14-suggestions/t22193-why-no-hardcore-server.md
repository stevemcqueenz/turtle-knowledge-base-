# Why no hardcore server?

## Post 153990 by Clintonm — 2025-10-02T05:13:00Z
<!-- url: https://forum.turtlecraft.gg/viewtopic.php?p=153990#p153990 | page 1 | era: pre-1.18.1 -->

I say this as a non-hardcore player.

Having hardcore players on the server doesn't help it's "population", as you can't group with them anyway. It's just annoying when you're both waiting for a quest mob to respawn and you can't group up.

I can also imagine as a hardcore player you'll find it annoying when you're trying to be careful in the open world when some non-HC andy comes through blasting mobs all over the place and then you pull too much argo accidentally amid the chaos.

Also, the thing about hardcore servers is that the population pyramid is quite different, in that there are far few people in higher level zones. That means that players that make it to higher levels _should_ have a large selection and easier time farming nodes etc. But being mixed in on a non-hardcore server I suspect ruins this dynamic.

Apparently HC players are really struggling to find groups [https://www.reddit.com/r/turtlewow/comm ... g_a_group/](https://www.reddit.com/r/turtlewow/comments/1nvi4pb/frustrated_while_making_a_group/), especially now they're split over two servers.

The server infrastructure/maintenance cost that may have been an argument against a HC before doesn't really apply anymore. Turtle WoW now has the expertise to spin up new servers, and this actually takes load of existing servers so it probably won't increase overall costs. It may actually decrease server costs as resources required sometimes increase superlinearly with load, so splitting HC into a new server may actually reduce costs (in any case I think the difference is trivial).

The immediate short term issue would be that just adding an extra server for HC arguably splits the HC player base even more. I think it would be okay in this case to offer free transfers from existing servers to the HC server. Then when characters reach level 60, if they want to remain HC they can stay on the HC server, but if they want to take immortality they can transfer back to Nord.

This seems like a no-brainer and win-win for all. Both HC and non-HC players will appreciate a separate server I think, the increased server costs will be minimal or possibly non-existent. I'm wondering the reason why NOT to do this?

## Post 154002 by galapagos (Barrens Chat Casualty) — 2025-10-02T08:10:00Z
<!-- url: https://forum.turtlecraft.gg/viewtopic.php?p=154002#p154002 | page 1 | era: pre-1.18.1 -->

I completely understand your reasoning — on paper, splitting HC players onto their own server looks like it would reduce frustration for both sides. The problem is that in practice a permanent HC server creates more issues than it solves, both for HC and non-HC players. Let me break down why this is the case.

 1) Community fragmentation and the “empty hall effect”
The biggest cost here isn’t technical, it’s social. Moving HC players to their own server immediately splits guilds, friend groups, and the economy. At launch, it feels busy and exciting, but HC by nature has a survival pyramid: the majority of characters don’t reach endgame. After a few weeks, the curve flattens and then declines — leaving very few high-level players, while mid-level zones become ghost towns. New HC players then log in and see a “dead world.” This problem is far worse on a separate server because there’s no steady flow of non-HC activity to keep the world looking alive.

 2) The “hard to find groups” issue is structural, not just population-based
HC grouping difficulties don’t stem only from numbers online, but from behavior: slower leveling pace, more cautious play, stricter group composition requirements, and the constant churn of characters dying. Even if you had an HC-only server, these same issues remain. People will still avoid risky PUGs, and characters will still disappear from the pool. A separate server doesn’t fix that; it just makes the gaps more noticeable when the initial hype dies down.

 3) Economy collapse: separate AH means less supply and more volatility
HC players rely on a stable flow of mid-tier mats, consumables, and crafted gear. On an isolated HC server, you end up with fewer suppliers, fewer alts, and fewer farmers. After the first month, prices swing wildly, consumables vanish from the AH, and the market feels unstable. On a shared server, non-HC provides the baseline activity that smooths these ups and downs. For HC, that stability is actually a benefit, not a drawback.

 4) “Non-HC interference in the open world” can be solved without a split
This frustration is real, but it doesn’t require a separate server. It can be solved through design tweaks:

shared quest credit / mob tagging in bottleneck areas,

dynamic respawn timers in hotspots,

soft layering (phasing) for HC-flagged characters during peak congestion,

small quest cooperation buffs (auto-shared credit without formal grouping).
These solutions directly address the annoyance without forcing a permanent wall between communities.

 5) “More herbs and ore for HC” is a short-lived illusion
Yes, with fewer competitors, HC players would initially have easier access to high-level resources. But over time, demand also drops (fewer raiders = fewer consumables needed) and supply becomes inconsistent (fewer dedicated farmers). That leads to unstable prices and scarcity. On a shared world, the broader population provides a healthier, more predictable market.

 6) Transfers “back and forth” create new problems
Offering free transfers sounds fair, but it creates a host of issues: drained populations on one server, messy edge cases (mail, AH, lockouts), and extra load on support. The “stay HC here, move to Nord at 60 if you want” model also undermines HC endgame — some players leave before HC guilds can solidify, which weakens the long-term HC ecosystem.

 7) Operational cost isn’t just spinning up hardware
Sure, the team knows how to create new shards, but running a parallel server isn’t just clicking a button. Each new server requires separate monitoring, event tuning, QA (since different population dynamics = different bugs), moderation, support, backups, and balancing. That overhead eats development and support bandwidth that could otherwise go into QoL improvements or events for everyone.

 8) Reputation risk: a “dead” server hurts more than temporary crowding
Players remember emotions, not raw numbers. If a new HC server looks active for the first 6–8 weeks and then feels deserted, that “dead server” reputation sticks forever. It discourages newcomers, weakens guild recruitment, and creates a downward spiral. By contrast, a shared server always looks alive.

 9) There are much better, less risky alternatives
Instead of a permanent split, HC frustrations could be solved through:

a dedicated /worldhc channel with better LFG visibility and filters,

temporary seasonal HC “events” (8–10 weeks) with rewards transferred to Nord afterward — synchronizing the HC population without long-term fragmentation,

smarter quest credit systems and dynamic spawns,

soft layering only for HC players in high-traffic zones,

small incentives to group in HC (e.g. one-time XP bonuses for dungeon clears),

in-game social tools (pin boards, tavern recruitment notices).
These strike at the root causes of frustration without splitting the player base.

 10) “It will reduce server load” isn’t guaranteed
Server load doesn’t scale linearly. Instances, scripts, and peak usage patterns don’t necessarily get lighter just because players are split. In fact, maintaining another shard adds overhead that can cancel out any supposed savings. The gain is minimal at best, while the social costs are high.

 11) Long-term, the quality of interaction matters more than server labels
HC thrives on trust, reputation, and reliable groups. A shared world with a stable economy and design tweaks to reduce friction gives HC players a stronger foundation. A permanent split looks attractive at launch, but in the long run it leaves HC players in a smaller, weaker ecosystem.

In summary
A separate permanent HC server only appears to solve a few short-term annoyances, but at the cost of splitting communities, destabilizing the economy, making group-finding worse after the initial hype, and adding operational overhead. The healthier approach is to keep everyone in one world while delivering targeted solutions (quest credit sharing, dynamic spawns, LFG tools, seasonal HC events, soft layering). That way HC frustrations are addressed at the root, without condemning players to a fragile, shrinking server that risks being labeled “dead” within months.
