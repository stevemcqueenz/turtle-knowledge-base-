# We need tweaks- not revamps

## Post 115910 by Bigsmerf (Grandmaster of Forum PvP) — 2025-01-08T17:34:00Z
<!-- url: https://forum.turtlecraft.gg/viewtopic.php?p=115910#p115910 | page 1 | era: pre-1.18.1 -->

I think it's safe to say we all know how CC2 went. I don't want to say it's been going bad- but, by my judgement, and many other's... It's been going *wrong*

What do I mean by this? Well- if you've been around for a couple years now, you might've heard of a special little philosophy and design thought process the turtle team used to operate by. In a nutshell, it revolved around keeping classes more or less the same in their general playstyle, and tweaking talents and abilities to improve the performance of previously less strong specs. When I say less strong I don't mean unviable, because believe it or not- just about everything was.

While I understand that the turtle team did indeed already add plenty of abilities or new talents, they were normally ones that either didn't have much impact on rotation, or gave fun utility and never changed the core of a specialization's identity. What he have now? Wellll...

Melee healer paladin, self destruct arcane mage, completely revamped survival hunter (which isn't bad in a vacuum, I've been loving it), support rogue, elemental shaman with no burst, hot streak lite fire mage, smite priest replacing discipline, feral cat being made into a bleed build that still performs worse than normal cat, new feral talents that only focus on bear or cat... And a strange self harm fury build that moves faster when it uses bloodthirst... Neat, but- is it at all necessary or even close to what people might consider 'vanilla'...?

Take a wild fucking guess.

Okay, okay- I've made my point. We're starting to stray off the beaten path, obviously. I don't want to say that's inherently a bad thing- as some of these changes can be fun and appealing in their own right, and seem like interesting design concepts, but... Again, the issue is- as many have pointed out... None of them are true to vanilla, which is what Turtle's playerbase seems to want. If balance is the issue, if could have very much been achieved through number tweaking, talent melding or other things along those lines. Boomkins doing low damage and having mana problems? Just make their spells do more damage and lower the mana cost. Enhancement shamans having threat issues? Lower threat on their DPS centric abilities. Prot pallies not having a taunt? Just give them a normal fucking taunt (which I think they did actually). Mana problems? Lower consecrate mana cost, or something.

What the devs don't understand (yes, it's not the other way around) is that we (for the most part) never wanted our specs completely revamped, torn apart, or nerfed in the hybrid department. Hell, some of us just wanted to play the same old meme and have fun doing it. I loved feral before CC2, but playing now just leaves a sour taste in the mouth, 'cause every talent and piece of tier gear I look at basically tells me "HEY, wanna do cat DPS? Hope you like this completely different playstyle we made for ourselves instead of you! Fuck off with that powershift crap, you just don't get it yet. Can't play bear without hurting your cat capabilities, either! Gotta pick one or the other pal!"

Might've turned this into an angy rant near the end here, but-... Eh, I'm sure we've all had our fair share of frustrations with how things are going. This is sort of a half vent half suggestion (that will never be seen) and I figured I'd get it out of my system.

Thanks for everything you guys do, though. I've thoroughly enjoyed my time here for what it's worth, and I think that despite everything I'll be poking my head back in every so often since A) I'm poor as fuck and this is free- and B) Turtle WoW is genuinely the best WoW experience I've ever had despite all its flaws and recent fuck ups.

I can give you a straightforward response. With only three posts on the forum and 250 days of playtime in the game, you can consider me part of the "silent majority." Here are the reasons I stopped playing on the TWoW server, leaving behind over 6,000 hours of effort. I'll provide detailed examples to clarify my points.

 1. The Game Has Changed
One thing I disliked about Blizzard's WoW was the rapid pace of content releases. If you still haven’t obtained the gear you want from Blackwing Lair, it doesn’t matter—here comes Ahn'Qiraj for you to farm instead. Only a small percentage of players have defeated Kel'Thuzad? No problem, let's move on to The Burning Crusade. I joined a private server to enjoy my favorite version of WoW indefinitely. I love the Vanilla version, its builds, and metas, which is why I sought out a Vanilla private server. TWoW was the best option with a decent population for 40-man raids. Unfortunately, the developers of TWoW changed too much: they altered class mechanics (like removing the Power Infusion priest build), adjusted the meta (nerfing the Blade of Eternal Darkness), and decreased difficulty (power creep after the CC2). The late-game content and gear, like the Kara40 raid, are not what I was looking for.

 2. Turtle is Not Turtle Anymore
The original "turtle" concept emphasized a slower pace. Leveling was challenging, raid resets were slow (weekly), and server changes, including bug fixes, were gradual. Now, playing feels like being on a rollercoaster ride. One day, you’re using a standard smite priest build; the next, you have off-GCD burst damage that lets you take down T3-geared tanks easily. The following days see fluctuations in your power, such as losing your ability to AoE farm with the Mara blade, or suddenly having BiS from trash Naxx rings and necks.

 3. My Favorite Build Was Removed
Throughout my 250 days of playtime, plus another 100 in Blizzard Classic, I mainly played as a healing Power Infusion priest. There were two main build choices: deep holy and deep discipline (PI). The original balance between these builds was appealing. Deep holy provided around 8% more healing throughput, while deep discipline offered 10% more mana and the Power Infusion support spell. The difference was minor, allowing players to choose their preferred style. However, CC2 eliminated the discipline build by removing the PI spell and nerfing healing throughput. Now, deep holy builds have about 35% more healing throughput, making them the only viable option for healing priests. If you’re playing as a healing priest, you have access only to healing spells. Personally, I prefer providing various forms of support beyond just healing. For example as Astrologian, in Final Fantasy XIV, you start as a healer, then gain expirience to use spells to boost your party reliable, ultimately leading to a card rotation for powerful buffs. I wish WoW had a similar concept, but the only support spell I enjoyed was removed. While the developers introduced a counterpart called Enlighten, its spell power boost is reduced by 50%, has a time limit of only 8 seconds, and is RNG-based. This means no skill or coordination is required with your DPS partner.

 4. Friends Are Leaving the Server
Many of the friends I made while playing are also leaving the server.

 5. Excessive Rare Drops
Vanilla WoW was somewhat luck-based; you might get Thunderfury on your first Molten Core run or farm it for a year. While it wasn’t satisfying to receive an item every day, lowering drop rates below a certain threshold can become frustrating. For instance, I participated in all the ES raids since their release on October 7, 2023, completing 60 runs without ever seeing the Scythe enchanting recipe drop. It’s not just this one recipe; other items, raid mounts, lower dungeon mounts, and the Snowball from loot boxes have ridiculously low drop chances. Eventually, you start to wonder, "Why am I even doing this?"

 6. Quest Progression is Deleted Unexpectedly
There’s an in-game title called "Seeker of Knowledge," awarded after completing 2,800 quests. The more quests you finish, the harder it becomes to progress. It’s incredibly time-consuming. Sometimes, you log in only to find that your quest completion counter has decreased, erasing a month’s worth of progress. You have no idea why this happened, but you suspect it’s due to undisclosed changes not mentioned in the changelogs.

 7. The New Content is Disappointing
Have you seen anyone who genuinely enjoys the ES raid? I haven’t. The new sleep mechanics are incredibly boring, as is clearing all the trash mobs. While the amount of trash has been reduced over time, and the sleep mechanic is now dispelable after a year, it’s still not enjoyable. Kara10 is fun for a while, but it’s disappointing when you compare the mechanics of Moroes here to those in TBC. The new raids aren’t crucial since only a few items are useful from them. However, the upcoming Kara40 raid is a different story. I fear it will be just as disappointing as other custom content and will shift the high-end gear meta, ultimately changing the entire game. With the CC2, you can see the power creep making other game aspects trivial. T3 gear is already overpowered, and T3.5 will be even more so. When you put all these factors together, you see a game that no longer appeals to me.

Having invested so much time in TWoW, it’s hard to just walk away. I notice change after change, each one stripping more fun from the game, and while I hope for improvement, these seven points make it difficult for me to justify logging in again. These are my personal reasons; I can understand that others may enjoy the new changes, just as some prefer the retail version or the Shadowlands expansion. However, one thing is certain: TWoW is no longer the Vanilla experience I loved.

No, "Vanilla design philosophy" is neither completely subjective nor useless talk. Vanilla design principles are objectively identifiable and analyzable. Game design principles are deliberate choices by developers to shape player experiences. Vanilla's design emphasizes the world, social interaction, and the journey, all of which are measurable through mechanics like slow leveling, somewhat challenging open world, and distinct classes. If you have played both Vanilla and Retail, it should be rather easy to see how they were designed differently (I pointed to some of the Vanilla principles in my previous post above). Even modern Blizzard correctly pointed out Vanilla design principles (they just failed to follow them):

[https://news.blizzard.com/en-gb/article ... ng-classic](https://news.blizzard.com/en-gb/article/23813563/developer-update-a-look-at-whats-ahead-for-wrath-of-the-lich-king-classic)

According to that article, this is what modern Blizzard themselves mention in regards to this:

 *Nurture and Protect Social Experiences
 *Approachable and Familiar
 *The World is the Main Character

This really isn't difficult. You only need to ask the question: "How does Vanilla WoW stand out compared to the later WoW expansions?":

 *The World is the Main Character:
 - The world doesn't care who you are; you're just a "nobody", fighting his way up through the world
 - The world is somewhat challenging
 - Emphasis on exploration; traversing the world takes a lot of time
 - The world doesn't bend itself around the player's convenience
 *Leveling takes a long time
 *Slower combat
 *Grindy, but rewarding
 *Classes are more distinct and you have to rely on other classes to a larger degree (class fantasy and class heterogenization)
 *Server community
 *Your reputation on the server matters
 *You have to talk to people to get things done
 *Gold matters
 *No needlessly complex system
 *Journey over destination
 *The players adapt to the game (generally)

 --->This can probably be condensed to just this:

 *The World is the Main Character
 *Social incentives over convenience and accessibility
 *RPG immersion emphasis
 *The journey is the reward

You can also flip the question: "How does the later expansions differ compared to Vanilla?".

 *Leveling is very quick and very easy
 *The open world is not challenging at all
 *Fast paced combat
 *Frequent updates to vertical progression through new levels or content, largely invalidating previous content
 *Multiple tiers of difficulty in instances, with the lowest tiers being very easy, while the upper tiers are challenging
 *Flying
 *Heirlooms
 *Automated Dungeon Finders
 *Transmog
 *Teleportation across the world instead of relying on Mages, Warlocks or FPs
 *Servers don't really matter anymore due to Cross Server tech
 *Gold doesn't really matter
 *Extensive cash shop
 *Mobile-game-like world quests
 *Class homogenization
 *Hyperfocus on balance
 *Every expansion or major patch usually has some sort of "system" or complexities that you have to learn and grind for, in order to keep the Monthly Active Users and Daily Login metrics high
 *Lack of community due to the Cross Server tech
 *Not particularly social due to the Cross Server tech, not having to talk to people to create groups (generally), not having to rely on other classes (class homogenization), and everything except instanced endgame content  being so soloable, thus not incentivizing to frequent socialization
 *The players being "Champions" from like level 10, instead of being foot soldiers
 *Destination over journey
 *The game adapts to the players (generally)

 --->This can probably be condensed to just this:

 *The Player is the Main Character
 *Convenience and accessibility over social incentives
 *Streamline away RPG-elements
 *Endgame is the focus

Sure, there were a lot of bullet points, but by abstracting over them, you can pull out the essence which makes up the design philosophy. What's the essence of Vanilla? What's the essence of Retail? If you've played both, you should know. It's really just Old School style vs. Modern style.

Why is design philosophy relevant for CC2?  Because when the class changes bring so much power creep it breaks Vanilla principles and thus moves the game closer to Retail philosophy:

 *It makes leveling faster and easier, and thus less rewarding, and less social because you don't need to rely on others as much when everything is so easy.
 *More class homogenization, which again makes the game less social as you don't need to rely on other classes as much. It also hurts class fantasy and class uniqueness.
 *When the players are made so powerful through these changes, the players start to become the main characters, not the world.
 *The changes were probably based on raid-performances, and thus endgame focused and neglecting other parts of the game.
...And there are many more reasons, as posters here have pointed out. The "Vanilla"-part of "Vanilla plus" would not make sense if the "Vanilla plus"  in its development breaks Vanilla design principles. Such a game could quickly end up as Retail 2.0.
