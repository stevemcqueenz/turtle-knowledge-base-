A Heavy Duty - Chapter 1: On the Road to Westridge.

==================================================================

Whatever else one might think of the Stonemasons, they knew how to build a road. League after league fell behind, as Standhaft and his companions cantered west through Elwynn.

The sun shone brightly, the sky blazed blue, and their journey from Goldshire had been surprisingly uneventful. Smoke rose ominously from distant fires, but the road itself was clear. Bandits and beasts undoubtedly did not wish to tangle with the armed party of mounted warriors.

They stopped every hour to rest the horses and Drago – and Balinda who was saddle sore – though Standhaft did not wound her feelings by saying so. He felt her hands around his waist and wanted to ask her how she was holding up. Balinda was not complaining, but he knew she was not a practised rider.

Standhaft glanced back to see how the others were faring. Drago was running along, perfectly happy and fit, and cousin Fergus caught his eye.

“Standhaft! I thought you could outride me by ten leagues,” he laughed.

“That’s right!” Standhaft teased. “I said I could. I didn’t promise I would.”

“Maybe you two can ride to Westfall in a single day, but I’m hungry,” Leoric complained.

“We all need a rest,” Standhaft told him. “Up ahead is the Fargobourne. It’s no more than a brook here, but it’s water. Marshal Dughan’s map shows a feeder spring not far from the highway. We’ll make Westridge long before sundown.”

Just before the bridge that spanned the stream, they found a worn track leading down to the water, and to fresh sweet grass for the horses. Standhaft jumped down from his tall Arathi destrier, Beast, and unwrapped the package of waybread and dried apples. Drago lapped at the stream, and then came to sprawl at his side, powerful jaws crushing the mealbar Standhaft tossed to him. Balinda followed, trying her best to keep her natural gait, and joined Standhaft in the shade of a willow; while Fergus and Leoric secured the horses before coming for their share of the meal.

It seemed an idyllic place. Water trickled sweetly down the stones of the spring, flowing into the Fargobourne on its journey to the Nazferiti River.

Standhaft ate hungrily, trying to make the food last as long as possible, savouring the apples’ smoky sweetness and the bread’s yeasty crunch.

“It’s hard to believe we’re at war,” Leoric murmured into the pleasant stillness.

“Three horses and a dog, but you’re still the dumbest member of the party, Leoric.” Fergus snarked.

“Thousands of refugees starving in Stormwind and you forget there is a war because of one babbling brook? How can you say something so thoughtless and stupid?” Balinda demanded, her voice unusually sharp.

Standhaft leaned back against the smooth bark of the willow, shutting his eyes, trying to enjoy the tickling breeze on his face. They were lucky in their weather and didn’t need this bickering so soon into their travels.

What would father do? He had commanded not only the House and the Barony, but legions of soldiers. In his final days, he had led the entire Alliance. Surely he would know how to manage three companions.

Fergus was too snide, but Standhaft valued his judgement. And Fergus was right, of course. What Leoric had said was unbelievably foolish. Three human kingdoms fallen, three more in crisis and near the brink, and the last, Stormwind, was barely doing any better. King Varian had sailed away never to return and outside the capital territory, the land was in chaos.

Balinda did not normally participate in these arguments, but she had fled the North in the wake of the Third War and come to Stormwind like many other refugees with nothing but the clothes on her back. In her case, she was not only a refugee, but also an orphan, as her family had died of sickness at sea.

But that Leoric had known peace, and still remembered it, was a good thing. And that was the point, wasn’t it? They wanted more people to be able to live in peace. This was the unifying point that Standhaft decided he must make. Standhaft took a deep breath and prepared to interrupt his squabbling companions.

Drago growled low and warningly. Instantly alert, Standhaft opened his eyes, and was on his feet. Fergus, Leoric, and Balinda had heard Drago too, and had stopped their bickering, looking about warily.

“What is it, boy?” Standhaft asked softly, “Bandits?”

He did not respond, and kept up his low rumbling growl. “Gnolls? Show me.”

The dog was glaring at a clump of bushes on the other side of the stream, his muzzle thrust forward aggressively. Standhaft eased his sword from its sheath. “Whoever you are,” he called, “Come out now with your hands empty and where I can see them!”

A smothered squeak, and the bushes shook violently. Light footsteps ran away into the undergrowth.

“Drago!” Standhaft shouted. “Go!”

The dog burst away, a blur of speed, splashing through the stream and tearing a path through the leaves. Standhaft followed him, but was only on the far bank of the stream when he heard the thin, high shriek.

“It’s a child!” Leoric cried, plunging after him.

Up the shallow slope, fighting past vicious brambles, they were on Drago and his prey almost too quickly to avoid trampling them. Drago had knocked the child down, and being too well-trained to hurt a small human, was simply holding him? – her? to the ground with his solid mass.

The child kicked out, screaming, “No! No! Mother! No! Help!”

“Let go, Drago,” Standhaft ordered, reaching for a skinny arm. Pulling the child up, he found that Drago had caught a boy of perhaps eight or nine, dressed in the rough clothes of a peasant. He shrank away from the imposing sight of the armoured men and their swords, and looked up timidly at Standhaft, not even trying to free himself.

“You shouldn’t sneak up on a warriors’ camp, boy,” Standhaft told him quietly. “They’re likely to think you’re an enemy. What are you doing out here all alone?”

The boy gaped at him. Standhaft gave the thin arm an impatient shake.

“Who are you, and what do you want?”

“Help,” he squeaked, fidgeting desperately. “We need help. Father didn’t come home. Mother won’t leave without him and something happened over at Old Thornby’s holding. I can’t find Father.”

“The land should be safe this close to the capital!” Leoric said, shaking his head.

Standhaft could only agree. He asked the boy, “How long has your father been gone?”

“Four nights now. Mother doesn’t know what to do.”

“She needs to go to Westridge,” Standhaft said crisply. “How far is your holding?”

“Not far – over that ridge,” the boy whimpered. “I thought maybe you’d help us. You didn’t look mean, but I was scared to show myself. There are bad men out here sometimes.”

“I daresay there are. Come along. We’ll go speak to your mother. And you didn’t tell me your name.”

His name, it transpired, was Owen, and he had never been on the back of a horse before. Fergus rolled his eyes, but did not even attempt to object to his presence or their detour. Fergus did nearly comment that three to a horse was a bit much, even on Beast, but then thought better of it when he realised the solution most likely involved either Balinda or Owen joining him on his own little Eastvale gelding.

Balinda helped Owen up first, who Standhaft seated in front of him, and then climbed up herself. They were heavy, but the boy didn’t add enough weight for Beast to complain.

“But Jack won’t like it if I up and go without telling him! What if he comes back and doesn’t know where we are?”
Standhaft silently thanked his father for making him come along on those boring visits to their tenants. He knew how to talk to this woman. Grecia, her name was: wife of Jack Hudson.

The house was small and tidy: a kitchen and sitting room combined, a door to a little bedroom, a door to the larder, and a ladder up to a loft where the elder children slept. Four children in all. Owen was the eldest, and then there were three girls like stair steps. The smallest could barely walk, and beamed at the dog, putting out chubby arms to him.

Standhaft said calmly, “You will leave him a note, telling him that you have gone to Westridge. You will pack the cart, hitch your oxen, and come along now.”

The pale and pregnant woman looked at him, lip trembling. “Jack doesn’t like when I do things without telling him.”

There was no time for this rubbish, “You can tell him you were commanded by the Baron of Blackwood to evacuate to Westridge. That is perfectly true. Now, get a move on!”

“Yes, milord!”

Standhaft fixed the women with his sternest glare. “My men and I will hitch the oxen for you. Take all the food in the house.”

“Sir – milord,” Grecia hesitated, “Are you sure there are gnolls out there? Jack says it’s just a story that Count Ridgewell put about to get his hands on the western holdings.”

“Pretty sure,” Leoric managed.

Fergus strolled into the little house, looking about in disdain. “Why are we wasting our time here? Let her find out for herself!”

The woman gaped at Fergus.

Standhaft hastily told her, “Yes, there are gnolls. We fought some only days ago. It’s no mere rumour. You are too close to their incursions, and too isolated for safety. I want to hear no more about it. Dress your children warmly.” That those gnolls had been east of Goldshire he neglected to mention.

There was no parchment in the house for a note. Standhaft dug some out of a saddle bag, and wrote “GONE TO WESTRIDGE'' in his largest, clearest hand. He felt a bit of satisfaction in nailing it to the table. Jack was likely dead, but there was a chance he yet lived and would re-join his family.

The boy and the eldest girl were more useful than their mother, who dithered over her belongings. The girl shooed their chickens into a little cage to be put in the cart, and let the sheep out into the pasture, so they would not starve. The boy was eager to be gone, and showed Standhaft a column of smoke rising above the trees.
“That’s Thornby’s place. It’s been burning all night.”

Fergus jeered, “Are we going to rescue him, too? Are we going to go from hut to hut to wretched hut, continually saving people who had not the sense to save themselves?”

“Of course, we have to do something to help!” Leoric instantly asserted. Balinda nodded her agreement emphatically, “How can you be so cold and heartless, Fergus? How can you turn your back on these poor people?”

“I’m not turning my back, I’m just being realistic. We can't go around helping every backwater we wander into. We'll be helping them all by restoring the Westfall breadbasket and preventing a famine. Are we to abandon our mission outright?”

They all looked to Standhaft to confirm their path. Imagining the fates of the people in this little valley of the Fargobourne made Standhaft ill, but he knew that Fergus was right.

“No,” he sighed. “We’re not. This is already going to cost us a day. We cannot save them all. We have a bigger mission that we need to stay focused on.” He thought that Fergus looked a bit too smug at his answer, and glowered. “But we shall save these people.”

The disappointment on the faces of Leoric and Balinda gnawed at him, as he packed candles and candlesticks, knowing that his mission to restore Westfall should take precedence over one farm family. To walk away, however, was almost certain to sentence them to death.

==================================================================

Leoric had hitched the oxen to the cart, and was uncomplainingly loading the little vehicle with crates and blankets, with a churn and a featherbed and two smoked hams. Fergus begrudgingly helped, but amused himself by sneering at the children. Balinda took the opportunity to gather dried herbs and flowers hanging from the ceiling.

“Silverleaf,” she murmured. “I know just what to do with that!”

The boy was leading the family’s milk cow, and tying her to the back of the cart. Their speed would be halved, at the best. They would almost certainly have to camp tonight. Standhaft rubbed the back of his neck irritably, the blood in his veins pounding with frustration. Drago stared out at the forest, ears alert. The wind was in the wrong direction for him to pick up any scents: blowing away from them toward the impenetrable dark green.

The smallest girl was a particularly cute child. She toddled up to Standhaft, huge blue eyes hopeful.

“Up!”

Grecia, fussing with her pots, called, “Annie, don’t bother milord!”

“I don’t mind,” Standhaft smiled, lifting the little one into his arms. The child nestled there, a warm bundle of life, her white-blonde hair silken and sweet. Standhaft thought with a pang about the possibility that this naïve quest might lead to his premature death. The idea that he might be the last of his bloodline made his temples ache with the pressure. He had always wanted at least two, himself, and he would name them –

Drago growled.

“Standhaft.” Leoric was looking at the forest line, tense and wide-eyed.

He was reaching for his sword…

In a flash, a dark cloud appeared which split into several humanoid figures, just to his right. Their silhouettes belied men, but their wolven heads and claws quickly disproved that. Wretched howling battle cries escaped the gnolls as they rushed towards the party. One charged toward Standhaft, gibbering, teeth bared in a mad grin.

His first impulse was to clutch the child to him, but that was wrong – all wrong – and at once his training came to him. Gritting his teeth, he tossed the wailing little girl aside, heedless of small hands scraping raw on his chainmail, and leaped at the monster, drawing sword and dagger with a smooth metallic whisper. His blades scissored, nearly severing the gnoll’s head.

A clang. Leoric, his shield propped against the wagon, had slammed a stewpot instead against the gnoll brute attacking him, knocking it to the ground. Drago rushed in, and bones snapped as the hound ripped away at the gnoll’s face.

Standhaft could see the pregnant woman framed in the doorway, hands over her mouth in horror, eyes wide and white with fear. The oxen lowed and stamped; the horses whinnied, shying away from the commotion. Wild, high shrieks filled the air, as the children darted in every direction, distracting the gnolls from the armed warriors. Beast squealed in rage, and lashed out with iron-shod hooves, scattering a gnoll’s head into scarlet splinters. Fergus seemed grateful for the horse’s assistance and hurried to guard against another gnoll brute’s attack.

A backhand and a squelching stab. Another gnoll grimaced in agony and fell back, twitching. Arrows thudded into the wagon, one passed an inch from his face, and Standhaft saw the leering gnoll archers, not ten yards away…

“Balinda!”

“I am here!”

A blast of cold frosted the creatures, slowing them. Fergus and Leoric were hacking at them before they could knock another arrow. Another gnoll was roaring, a little further on, gibbering some incantation. Sparks suddenly leaped around it, but Standhaft was on it, cutting its throat in a veil of blood before it could cast. But there was another one, half-hidden by the encroaching forest –

Roots sprang from the earth, grabbing at him, and he nearly tripped. Staggering forward, he glared at the chuckling gnoll caster, its staff raised high for another spell.

“Less casting, more dying!” yelled Leoric. His longsword flashed in a steel arc, and quite suddenly the gnoll caster was headless, blood pumping from its thick neck. It fell forward, spraying Standhaft.

He groaned in disgust, and shook off the last of the spell, the thick roots rapidly withering and dying. Behind him, the mother was screaming, a horrible hoarse sound. Standhaft wiped foul blood from his eyes. The last of the gnolls had made a grab for Annie, sitting sobbing in the bloody ground. Balinda had frosted the creature, weakening it. Standhaft vaulted a low wall while Drago charged, bowling the gnoll over. The hound gnawed at the gnoll’s wrist, forcing it to drop its crude axe. Standhaft stabbed down, nailing the monster to the earth. It thrashed wildly, gobbling and choking. Standhaft twisted his blade, and the gnoll jerked and was still.

“Gnolls!” Owen remarked, unnecessarily, crawling out from under the cart. He poked at a dead gnoll with a bare foot.

“Don’t touch it!” Standhaft ordered. “If you have any blood on you, wash it off immediately! Grecia! Do you hear me? Make sure the children haven’t any gnoll blood on them! These filthy creatures carry disease!”

He crouched down by little Annie, to see if she were badly hurt. The child stared at Standhaft with her huge blue eyes, and uttered a high, piercing shriek. Her mother rushed up to gather her in her arms, and the other children emerged from their hiding places to huddle all together.

“Come children!” the woman choked, “Let’s have a look at you!” She dabbed at them, now and then glancing up at the party a little fearfully.

Fergus cast a look over the carnage. “Perhaps we ought not to linger?” he suggested.

Leoric came over to admire Standhaft’s handiwork. “I think we work well together,” he quipped. He grinned at Standhaft. “You’ve, uh, got something on your face.”

Standhaft tried to laugh, and failed miserably. “I must wash this off quickly. Could you finish loading that blasted cart so we can get out of here?”

He stumbled over to the well, with Balinda in pursuit, and hauled up a bucket. “Here.” He shoved the bucket at her, and leaned over. “Pour it over my head before it congeals.”

“Of course. You do look a sight.”

It took two more buckets before he was clean of the worst of it. Balinda seemed inclined to continue grooming Standhaft, and he might have allowed it under different circumstances, but he brushed her off and declared that it was time to leave.

Balinda bristled at her dismissal, but obeyed, and lingered just long enough to burn the bodies. “Ashes cannot rise again,” she whispered to herself, arcane fire making quick work of her task.

Grecia and the girls were too stunned and terrified to do much more than they were told. Owen was almost too busy – panicky and wild-eyed. No one wanted to stay. The gnolls all lay where they fell, and Grecia only begged to be allowed to lock the door of the house.

Fergus actually laughed aloud, “She’s locking the gnolls out?”

The animals were restless and jittery, but calmed down as they put distance between themselves and the farmhold. Standhaft hoped that no other gnolls were near. With the noise the chickens alone made, they were a target for any predator in the neighbourhood. The children rode in the cart huddled with their mother, too afraid to be any nuisance. The party rode slightly ahead of the cart, so they could speak privately.

“That was stupid of me,” Standhaft muttered bitterly.

“What?” protested Balinda. “You did great!”

“Yeah! We are a glorious party of mighty gnoll slayers.” Leoric added.

“I was distracted by that child – playing when I should have been alert.” Standhaft responded.

“Yes, you were stupid,” Fergus agreed tartly. “Mooning over peasant children! Let it be a lesson to you about the dangers of sentimentality. And yes, Leoric, I agree that we are, in fact, rather glorious and mighty.”

“Whoa!” Leoric laughed. “We agree about something!”

“Don’t expect it to happen again.” Fergus quipped.

Standhaft was still stirred up and blew out an annoyed breath. They said little after that, and concentrated on getting as far as they could before the sun was low in the sky. The children napped, and then had to be given some bread to eat. And then they needed to stop and go – very cautiously – into the bushes with their tired mother.

Standhaft would have liked to offer to drive the cart for her, but he must stay exactly where he was. They would have to stop and camp when Grecia was too exhausted to hold the reins anymore. And Standhaft knew he must not offer to let any of the children ride with him. He must be alert.

As the day wore on, Owen got out and walked, and Jenn, the eldest girl, walked with him a good part of the way. The children might be wary of the warriors, but gravitated to Drago’s vicinity. He was happy enough for the company, and did not object to a small hand scratching his ears now and then.

So they camped early, by a nameless little stream with good water for their animals. There was much work to be done: unsaddling the horses, unloading the packs, watering and feeding the livestock, keeping track of the children. Owen gathered wood for the fire, and Balinda started it with a casual wave of her staff. She then withdrew to allow Grecia to cook for them.

Standhaft was passing by the fire, when Grecia called to him, very low.

“Milord!”

“Yes? What can I do for you?”

“That woman – ” Grecia nodded in Balinda’s direction. “Is she a witch?”

Standhaft forbore to frown at this ignorant yokel. Half-truths and obfuscation would work. “Balinda is a mage. There is nothing for you to fear. There are a number of mages – all approved by the Church and the Crown – in honourable service to the Kingdom. We are very fortunate to have Balinda with us, don’t you think? She did wonders to protect the children.”

“I suppose so,” the woman answered reluctantly. “But a wit – mage… what if she does something to us?”

“She has no reason to do anything except to continue to serve bravely. I give you my word that she is a friend and quite safe.”

“If you say so, milord.”

The woman went back to her cooking, only half convinced.

Standhaft sighed, and walked away to help Leoric and Fergus with the animals. Leoric was currying Beast.

“You’re quite good at that,” Standhaft observed after a moment.

“I should be,” Leoric grinned. “If I didn’t become your valet, I would have stayed a stableboy.”

“But you’re not my valet, you’re my squire, and my friend.”

Fergus looked as if he were about to say something immensely witty, and unnecessarily cruel, but Standhaft glared at him and he remained silent.

Grecia made them a good and plentiful supper – almost plentiful enough for Standhaft, Leoric, and Fergus. The thick porridge-like stew of barley and carrots and smoked mutton filled all the voids left by the exertions of the day. There was pure spring water to drink and apples to munch afterward. Fergus decided to take the first watch – Standhaft suspected to avoid the pandemonium of the children’s bedtime. The three oldest children had got over the worst of their fright, and sat close to the party, whispering about gnolls.

All but Annie. The little girl would not come near Standhaft. The little girl, in fact, would turn her head away from him, mouthing distorting into whimpers of fear. Standhaft swallowed a lump of misery and dug into his bags for something to lift morale.

“Cake!” he announced. “We’ll all feel better for a piece of plumcake.”

“Great idea!” Balinda seconded.

The precious cake, which Standhaft had intended for a later date when luxuries would be harder to come by, was unwrapped and cut into generous wedges. He set one aside for Fergus, on guard just beyond the trees.

“Oh, milord!” Grecia reached out uncertainly for the proffered treat. “Thank you kindly! Here, Jenn, give Alice this piece.”

“Do you know any stories?” Owen asked.

“A few,” Standhaft admitted.

“What about that ‘Be bold, but not too bold’ story? Leoric suggested.

“Not tonight,” Standhaft said easily. To Leoric, he mouthed the words, “Too scary.” He considered for a moment. Looking at Drago, he decided on a tale his father had often told him.

Long ago, before our fathers’ fathers came down from the Highlands, a war hound was born to the elder bitch of a tribal chief. They named him Escanor and gave him everything. He grew up a fine, strong pup, destined to be the partner of the chief’s eldest son. Escanor grew prideful. The young hound became arrogant, taking food from his kin and warning them – in the way of dogs – that the chief’s family would punish them if they tried to attack him. Years passed, and the time for the chief’s son to take a war hound came closer.

Escanor’s pride swelled, and many people of the tribe came to the chief, quietly whispering of his dog’s bullying. With each complaint, the chief saw only Escanor’s strength and pride, and sent his people away. But as his son grew, the chief watched more closely. The day might come when his boy’s life would depend on this dog. If the humblest of his people would not trust Escanor, how could he?

When the day came, Escanor sat proudly, waiting to be called. But the old chief chose Escanor’s brother as his son’s hound. Escanor was shamed, but felt no remorse. So great was his rage that he darted across the fire pit and bit the chief’s hand. The chief and his son struck at Escanor, cursing him. The hound ran into the village, seeking shelter in the tents and kennels. The other dogs snapped at him, and the tribe’s people threw stones at him. Before the chief could reach him, the tribe had torn Escanor apart.

Standhaft had heard the tale many times as a young lad. Father had told him many such tales, knowing that a noble needed to understand what price one could pay for their hubris. Father had made mistakes, but the loyalty of his men had never wavered and they had followed him into the pits of hell in the end.

It was Standhaft’s duty to lead men into battle, to rule over and care for his people. If they lost faith in him because of his personal pride or abuse of his power, not only would he destroy the ancient legacy of his family, but he would damn all who followed him as well.

“– And what is the moral of this story?” He asked the children.

“Don’t bite important people?” Alice ventured.

Balinda stifled a giggle and Leoric choked on his cake. Standhaft glared at him.

“Don’t be a bully?” This from Owen.

“That’s right!” Standhaft answered, giving the helpless Leoric another look. “You should never abuse your power. The strong must not take advantage of the weak.”

“And if the strong are mean, the weak people remember it,” Jenn said solemnly. She elbowed Owen.

“You are very clever children,” Standhaft told them. “And Alice, you are right. Biting important people is a very bad idea. I’m sure Drago would agree with that.”

Drago gave a considering rumble, cocking his head. Standhaft laughed. “You wouldn’t bite King Varian, would you?”

Leoric smirked, “Only if he bit you first.”

Drago barked happily, in complete agreement.

“Milord,” Grecia asked shyly, “have you seen King Varian with your own eyes?”

Standhaft answered in the affirmative, confirming such details as the King’s height and build, and his famous royal armour, and with regret, that King Varian was not in Stormwind but gone across the sea to a land called Theramore. Luckily, to this woman, Stormwind was as far away as Theramore and so it made no difference.

Grecia told Standhaft earnestly, “I’ve raised my children to honour the King! And to worship the Light and the Church, of course,” she added.

The older girl, Jenn, wanted to hear about the Prince.

“He’s young and healthy,” Standhaft told her, wishing to talk about something else. “He has golden hair and he likes to read.”

“Did you see the Queen, too?” asked Jenn.

Standhaft shook his head, “The Queen died many years ago – rest her soul – and she lies buried in the royal crypt.”

Leoric gave a nod, “There are lots of paintings and even statues of the Queen. She was very pretty. Tall and blonde and looks like a Queen ought to look.”

“Was she nice?” Owen asked.

Standhaft considered. “She was known to be kind-hearted and fair-minded,” he allowed.

“Such a lot of places the two of you must have seen!” Grecia marvelled. “You’ve both been to Stormwind? Really?”
At their amused looks, she asked, “Is that where you’re from? I heard it’s bigger than Westridge. There are hundreds of houses and thousands of people living there. It’s hard to believe.”

“It’s true that there are thousands of people in Stormwind,” Standhaft assured her, “and I was born in Stormwind, but Balinda and Leoric are originally from Lordaeron, and Fergus is from Stromgarde.

“Lordaeron! Stromgarde!” Grecia gasped, as astonished as she would have been by the name of some fabled land – Northrend or Kalimdor. “That’s all the way across the sea!”

“You can cross by land as well, but sailing is fastest,” added Balinda.

“Have you seen the sea, then?” Owen wanted to know.

With a rush, Standhaft pictured a summer’s day on the Storm Cliffs, the grey vastness of the South Seas glittering below, the stiff salt breeze, the scent of fish and the cries of the seabirds, the sun hot on his face, the prickleweed and the madcap trailing over the stony verge, the feeling that he and Balinda stood alone at the edge of the world...

“Yes.” He summoned a smile, pleased to see Balinda smile as well. “I have seen the sea. But enough talk! I think it’s time for little warriors to get some sleep!”

The children laughed. All but little Annie, who still would not look at him.

==================================================================

It took forever to get started in the morning. In the end, an irritated Fergus deposited the two younger children into the wagon half-dressed, while Grecia obsessively scrubbed at her pans and spoons. At least they had had a good breakfast, a fry-up of eggs and potatoes and wild greens. Grecia was a fine cook, if a slow one. The Hudson family seemed better for a night’s sleep.

“Let’s wear our tabards into the town,” Standhaft said to the party. “We might get a bit more cooperation that way.”

“Fine with me.” Leoric liked wearing his Blackwood tabard. The iconic black leafless tree on a golden background with black and gold borders. He thought it looked like the sun was shining bright on a new day that marked the end of winter. A New Dawn.

And the children seemed impressed by the finery. The tabards really were – neat.

“We’re going to Westridge!” Jenn cried to her sisters. “We’re going to see the town!”

The children besieged their mother with questions. They had never seen such a thing as a town, but their mother had visited Westridge not once but three times, and felt herself wise enough in city ways to prepare them for their adventure.

Their party grew as they met other wayfarers along the road. The first additions were a pair of frightened church brothers, hoping to be invisible in their hooded cloaks, clinging to the low stone walls along the road for cover, greeting the party on horseback with equal parts respect and caution.

A family of hill dwarves was swept up in their wake: husband, wife, and stocky little son, carrying their worldly goods on their backs. Grecia and her children gaped at them, and Jenn ran up to touch the little dwarf boy. Then she ran back to the shelter of the creaking wagon, pleased at her own daring.

“My lord,” the dwarven father asked Standhaft, civil and humble. “Would you permit us to travel under your protection? We will give you no trouble.”

“Of course you may.” Standhaft wondered how these dwarves are wandered so far south. Perhaps they had fled the troubles in the North like so many others. It was a serious undertaking to travel so far, and as many other refugees discovered, Stormwind was not without its own strife.

He caught the relieved look the parents shared. They each gave a hand to their little boy, and followed at the back of the party.

Behind him, Grecia hissed at Owen. “Keep an eye on those half-men. They’re like to steal the cow when we’re not looking!”

An elderly couple with a handcart was overtaken, not two miles from the town ahead, as the road began its descent into the valley of Nazferiti River.

“There are bandits on the road, sir,” the old man warned Leoric. “Neighbour of mine was robbed by ‘em on his way to town! And beaten, too!”

“But you’re on the road to Westridge,” Leoric pointed out mildly.

“No help for it, sir! No help for it. We can’t stay, and that’s the Light’s truth! We’ve got a bit put by, and we thought it might be enough for ‘em to let us through. But with you and your noble lord…”

“Right. Two more for Westridge!”

“Where did your neighbour encounter the bandits?” asked Standhaft.

“Not a mile from the town, on this side of the river. Reckon we’ll see ‘em soon enough, if they haven’t left for greener pastures.”

Another good thing about horses. One could see farther. Standhaft and Leoric scanned the road ahead for possible threats. It was not long before they spotted one.

“There they are,” Leoric pointed.

Standhaft saw the men lounging in the distance. Four – no – five fit and well-armed men, who by rights should be in the army. They had blockaded the road with overturned wagons and scattered crates and barrels.

“Stay together and keep up,” he told his charges. “These men are nothing to be feared.”

“They are fools to get in our way,” Fergus agreed.

Considering that they were three horses and fifteen people, he half expected the waiting men to melt into the trees and wait for weaker prey. But past success must have given them confidence. Their leader looked up at their approach, and came forward, a handsome young man with a cocky grin.

“Wake up, gentlemen!” he called, “More travellers approaching! And I’d say the tall dark one is the leader!”

The bandit’s biggest stooge gaped at the approaching party, and rumbled, “Uh, dey don’t look like dose odders. And dey’ve got a big dog. And horses.”

“Right, horses.” Fergus mimicked. “You should have seen what Beast there did to a gnoll’s skull yesterday.”

Leoric mimed an explosion. “Boosh! What a mess.”

The bandits edged away nervously.

Standhaft said, “Lucky for us you’re here. This road is a disgrace. It’s a wonder anyone can get through to Westridge.”

“Well, we let a few through, now and then,” the leader smirked. “If the price is right.”

“Oh? And what are you? Road guards?”

“Yes!” The leader grinned in delight, white teeth flashing. “That’s it exactly. We’re road guards! We tax the odd passing traveller to cover our expenses.”

“Well, I am the Baron of Blackwood, and I don’t pay taxes.”

“Blackwood?” The stooge blurted out, “Dat’s him! Dat’s da Black Knight! He’s da one dat killed dose ogres and orcs! Dey was talkin’ about him at da tavern!”

The bandits backed away a little further. The leader’s smile grew forced. Standhaft’s smile was forced, too. That bloody awful nickname had preceded him. Some idiot courier had babbled it out, and even these scum had heard it. The leader was looking at him with wary respect.

“The Black Knight, eh? The Hero of Northshire, I hear. Well, let’s forget about the tax. We’ll stand aside and let you get on with your orc-fighting and ogre-killing ways.”

“As soon as you clear the road,” Standhaft told him, perfectly seriously. Using that stupid nickname called for some degree of punishment.

“Ah – that’s not really how it works… ”

“Yes. That’s exactly how it works. I am shocked at the condition of the road you’ve been guarding. How are the army couriers to get through? How is a lord to get through? Whoever made this mess must be a traitor to Stormwind. I think it would be an act of patriotism to clean it all up.” He drew his sword and tested its edge. “I’m waiting.”

It was astonishing how fast the rubbish went over the sides of the road. The bandits all but polished the stones. Their leader grinned gamely, giving them a sweeping bow. Standhaft waved their followers on and stayed to speak to the man.

“You know,” he said, his voice light and conversational, “I’ve met many old soldiers. I’ve met many rich old soldiers. I’ve even met a number of rich old mercenaries.” He leaned down and smiled grimly. “But I have never met an old bandit. Ever. I suggest you rethink your path. There are opportunities for able men in these troubled times. But if I were to find you collecting taxes here again, I might misunderstand the situation and – lose my temper.”

“Right,” the man said slowly. “No old bandits.”

“Not even middle-aged.” Fergus mocked.

Standhaft turned his horse’s head and kicked it into a gallop, the party following behind their little train.
The stooge called out after him. “Did you really kill dat ogre? Dat’s pretty neat!”

==================================================================

“There it is. Westridge: pretty as a painting,” said Leoric, with a wave at the sight unfolding.

Standhaft agreed that it was quite a pretty place indeed. Close to the stone road leading directly into Westridge was a green meadow, where several tents were pitched. The camp it formed was sprawling and haphazard, and had no palisades or defences. Clearly not an army camp.

Beyond that, in the town proper, was a Church of very respectable size, a stone bridge over a stream that flowed into the Nazferiti River furthered down, some wattle and daub houses, and all the usual appurtenances of a country town. Further out, a mill loomed high above the rest on a rocky hill. And further yet, up a gentle rise, was a largish wood and stone edifice, surrounded by outbuildings, that must be Count Ridgewell’s manor.

He turned in the saddle to their new acquaintances. “You should all be safe enough now. Light keep you safe.”

But he and the others could not just ride away. Everyone wanted to thank the party, and Owen wanted to shake their hands, and the two older girls wanted to hug scout and kiss their protectors – Standhaft, Leoric, and Balinda, at least, as Fergus refused in disgust – and the dwarves bowed in their respectful and dignified way. The old couple waved their farewell cheerily, and the brothers quoted a blessing, then hurried off to the secure stone bulk of the Church.

“You could have been one of them,” Standhaft pointed out primly to Leoric. “Don’t you ever wonder if you’ve made a mistake?”

“I do not,” he answered with careless swagger. “I get to wear a noble house tabard and ride a horse. And besides, I never would have been a brother. They would have trained me as a paladin, and I would have had the spiffiest armour in the Eastern Kingdoms. No dull robes for me, thank you!”

“No,’ drawled Fergus. “It’s only your wits that are dull!”

“Oh, yes. Thank you so much for that insight, Fergus.”

There was a defensive wall around Westridge, Standhaft noted, but the wooden palisade was really no more than a fence. There was a wooden watchtower, currently unoccupied. There were no guards, and the flimsy gate was open.

The number of campers in the commons and the number of wagons must be unusual. People came to see the party and their horses, a fairly notable sight in these parts. There was gossip, and pointing fingers, and to Standhaft’s slight annoyance, a very distinct call of –

“That’s the Black Knight! You can tell by the black tree and his enormous size!”

Leoric burst out laughing. “All that hard work, but in the end all anyone sees is a big –” He cut himself off, stifling his laugh apologetically.

Standhaft frowned, but did not rebuke him. People were simple creatures, and at least it was positive gossip. “Let’s find a tavern and hear the news before we go on up to the manor.”

Almost immediately, it became clear that at Llane’s Refuge, they would never have to pay for their own drinks. Even after the welcomes and the cheers, and even after Standhaft’s polite greeting, people kept staring at them in a very unsettling way, apparently expecting them to do something prodigiously heroic at any moment.

“You know,” Leoric considered. “If we drink everything that people want to buy for us, we probably won’t be able to get back on our horses ever again.”

“Probably not, so don’t.” Standhaft agreed. The ale at Llane’s Refuge was very good, all the same. Music thrummed pleasantly from a gallery above where a group of minstrels played.

Fergus raised his brows with haughty languor, just barely containing his thoughts on what real music was, and sipped at a cup of pear wine from a goggling admirer.

Even Drago was growing tired of the adulation, and hid under their table alone.

“Get off my foot, filthy mongrel!” Fergus scolded.

“Fergus! Behave.” Standhaft admonished, beckoning Drago to come over for an ear scratching.

Only De Vries the barkeep appeared to be sane. He kept pouring drinks, muttering to the other patrons to leave the little party at the back table alone.

Out of the blue, Balinda remarked, “You know, I've always found it fascinating how different people react to alcohol. The way it affects their motor skills, inhibitions, and even their decision-making abilities. It's quite an intriguing study in human behaviour. I once read a book on the subject that delved into the physiological and psychological effects of alcohol consumption. Did you know that the rate at which alcohol is metabolised…”

Fergus’ blue blood stirred as Balinda began babbling about matters which he had no understanding of. All of his noble education, and this commoner had developed a level of understanding of matters organic that he simply had only the most rudimentary comprehension. He turned his attention to his cup of pear wine and tried not to let his insecurity show.

Leoric’s expression was less masked. He rarely had any idea what Balinda was talking about when she started… well, whatever it was she was doing. It might as well have been magic when she talked too, as far as Leoric understood it.

“It’s very interesting, I agree,” Standhaft replied, more to his own thoughts than anything else. Balinda’s intellect was almost as invaluable an asset as her raw arcane powers. If they had the leisure and liberty, Standhaft would have enjoyed nothing more than to follow Balinda down these rabbit holes, merely enjoying the curious nature of the world. But duty pressed him, as always. “Excuse me for a moment,” he said, and walked over to the bar.

“I was hoping to hear the news,” he said to De Vries. “Have you heard any rumours?”

“Let’s see…” The barkeep considered. “Do you want to hear the rumours about the Black Knight who saved Northshire from an ogre raid single-handed?” He gave Standhaft a wink.

“I – don’t think so.”

“How about the rumours that the King of Kul Tiras is really a naga in disguise?”

“Hmmm.” Standhaft smiled in spite of himself, but shook his head.

The barkeep pursed his mouth, and continued, more seriously. “Well, I heard that the Countess Edith, the young Gilnean wife of Count Erlgadin, was cheating on her husband with his brother Lord Janos, and that she poisoned the Count so that she and the brother could marry each other!”

Standhaft was sober quite suddenly. “You don’t say?”

 *Of course, that could be just as ridiculous as the rumours about me.*

“And folks up north say that another orc invasion is coming. New Redford was sacked and pillaged by the orcs. Hard to believe.”

“Believe it,” Standhaft said shortly.

“And there’s always the nasty rumour that the king has died at sea. The Light has forsaken him, they say, for abandoning the kingdom.”

“That’s very… unkind,” Standhaft replied slowly. “And I am sure King Varian will return soon.”

“And then what about the people just vanishing from their farmholds?” De Vries offered. “Just up and disappearing, no one knows where!”

“I know where. Gnoll raids.”

 *“And the Stormwind Army ignoring it!”* he thought to himself.

“Thank you for the drinks, but we must be going!” Standhaft turned and saw a dwarf man and an elf woman in Church garbs heading his way. From their earnest, hopeful expressions, Standhaft could tell they wanted something.

“We must all be going!” Standhaft repeated, more loudly for the party’s benefit. Drago was instantly at his heels. Fergus quickly downed his wine. Balinda shut her book and rose lithely from the little table. Leoric gave him a sly grin, but moved to the door nearly as fast.

“You know,” he muttered as they mounted their horses. “I smelled mutton roasting. If we’d stayed, we probably could have had a free meal!”

“And so we shall – at the manor,” Standhaft said. “Perhaps people won’t gawk at us so!”
